My Coxeter Group Naming System

A long time ago, I saw a page on Dr. Richard Klitzing's polytope website that presented an unusual naming system for spherical Coxeter groups, the basis of Wythoffian polytopes. The page refered to what most people (and Wikipedia) would call An, Dn, Bn, I2(p), En, F4, and Hn as A(n), B(n), C(n), D(2)p, E(n), F(4), and G(n) respectively.

In spite of the normally tribal attitude I held towards polytope naming schemes at the time ("The names I learned are good and everyone else's are bad and wrong!"), I immediately became attracted to the aesthetics of this alternate naming scheme. In particular, it had always frustrated me that the "traditional" names Bn and Cn to refer to the same Coxeter group. Those names were associated with two different Lie groups which corresponded to the same Coxeter group. The normal naming scheme also used G2 to refer to hexagonal symmetry, rather than giving it a systematic name like higher polygonal symmetries, which I thought was weird because it didn't really have a higher-dimensional equivalent (even though I now understand it's because the hexagon has special connections to Lie groups that higher polygon don't). The alternative naming system solved both of these issues.

Later I saw the same system in H. S. M. Coxeter's book Regular Polytopes, which may have been where it originated. I also found another set of names for Euclidean Coxeter groups using letters from P to W (Wikipedia says these are the Witt symbols named after Ernst Witt), while the normal names used Ã through G̃ (with tildes) for the same groups. I liked the fact that these two alternative systems for spherical and Euclidean groups used two ranges of letters from the same alphabet, and I wondered if there was an extension of this system that used the remaining letters for hyperbolic groups.

Of course, there are more compact and paracompact hyperbolic groups than there are remaining letters in the alphabet after A through G and P through W are removed. So I thought that some could get single letters and the rest would get double letters.

It turns out that the standard way to refer to hyperbolic groups, as given in this Wikipedia revision, is by a system of letters and pairs of letters with bars or hats on top, invented by Norman Johnson, called Extended Witt symbols. This system seems to use all the remaining letters, except for I (which is used in the traditional system to represent polygonal symmetry). I figured I could adapt those letters into my system and use I to refer to arbitrary 2D hyperbolic tiling symmetries, the same way D (or I in the traditional system) represents arbitrary polygon symmetries. I would want to remove the bars to match the symbols for Euclidean and spherical symmetries. Unfortunately, the other single letters with bars on top already had non-bar equivalents that referred to Euclidean and spherical symmetries, so I had to rename them.

A few more notes about the table: the symbols for the spherical groups are from Coxeter (I think) and the symbols for Euclideans are from Witt. I adopted them as part of "my" naming scheme, but the only symbols I actually invented were for hyperbolic groups. Even then, most of the symbols were inspired by Johnson's Extended Witt symbols, but some of them had to be altered to not clash with other symbols.

In the original two-letter Extended Witt symbols, there was a bar or a hat over both letters. But that was hard to transcribe in HTML, so I put two separate bars or hats over each letter.
